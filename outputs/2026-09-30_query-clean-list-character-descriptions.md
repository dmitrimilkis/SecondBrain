## 15 famous books

### 1. The Odyssey (Homer)

**Odysseus** · *Ulysses (as in this translation), son of Laertes, king of Ithaca*  
The "sagacious" and "much-enduring" king of Ithaca, famous as "quick in shrewd devices," who wanders for years after the fall of Troy trying to get home. He tricks the Cyclops by saying "Noman is my name," and returns "in the twentieth year" disguised by Pallas as a wrinkled old beggar ("thy locks Of auburn I will cause to fall away"). His old nurse recognizes him by a boar-tusk "scar" on his leg. After the suitors all fail to bend his great bow, he bends it "with ease," shoots an arrow through twelve rings, and kills the suitors.

**Penelope**  
Odysseus's faithful, clever wife, "the sage Penelope," besieged by greedy suitors. She promises to choose one when she finishes a shroud, weaving it by day while "every night Unravelled it by torchlight," for three years. When Odysseus returns, she tests him with the secret of their bed, which he built around the trunk of an olive tree.

**Telemachus**  
Odysseus's son, a newborn when his father left for the war. Urged by Pallas disguised as Mentor, he stands up to the suitors and sails to Pylos and Sparta for news of his father, then fights beside him against the suitors.

**Athena** · *Pallas / Minerva in this translation*  
"Pallas, the blue-eyed goddess," Odysseus's divine protector. She argues his case before the gods, visits Telemachus "in the shape of Mentor," and disguises Odysseus as a beggar with a touch of her wand.

**Polyphemus** · *the Cyclops*  
"Huge Polyphemus," a one-eyed Cyclops, a man "Of giant bulk" who lives alone with his flocks, the son of Neptune. He devours Odysseus's men "like a lion of the mountain wilds," until, while he sleeps off his wine, they heat a stake in the coals and drive it into "the single eye of Polyphemus." When the other Cyclops ask who is hurting him, he shouts "'tis Noman."

**Circe**  
Called "the fair-haired Circè," a goddess on the island of Aeaea, daughter of the Sun, who drugs Odysseus's men and turns them into pigs "in head and voice, Bristles and shape, though still the human mind Remained." Protected by the herb Moly, Odysseus makes her change them back.

**Calypso**  
"Calypso of the radiant hair," a goddess who keeps Odysseus on her island for seven years and offers to make him "immortal," until the gods order her to let him go.

**Argus** · *Argos, Odysseus's dog*  
Odysseus's old hunting dog, whom he raised before leaving for Troy. Once used for hunting, he now lies "Neglected" on the dung heap before the stable doors, "devoured with vermin." He lifts his head and pricks his ears at his disguised master, then dies "as soon as he had seen Ulysses, absent now for twenty years."

**Eurycleia** · *the old nurse*  
The faithful old nurse, bought by Laertes "for a hundred beeves" (cattle) when young, who nursed Odysseus and Telemachus. Washing the beggar's feet, she finds the scar and recognizes her master.

**Antinous** · *a leader of the suitors*  
"Antinoüs, Eupeithes' son," who with Eurymachus is "eminent above the others" among Penelope's suitors. She calls him "mischief-plotter, insolent!" He mocks Telemachus and assaults the disguised Odysseus. He is the first suitor killed, "slain by an arrow from the bow of Ulysses."

### 2. Hamlet (William Shakespeare)

**Prince Hamlet**  
The Prince of Denmark, son of the late king and nephew of the new one ("mine uncle is king of Denmark"), a student at Wittenberg still dressed in mourning black ("my inky cloak"). When his father's ghost reveals he was murdered, Hamlet decides to "put an antic disposition on" (pretend to be mad) and agonizes over whether to act ("To be, or not to be"). Ophelia mourns his lost gifts, "The courtier's, soldier's, scholar's, eye, tongue, sword"; the gravedigger's dates make him thirty. He kills Polonius and, finally, Claudius, and dies of Laertes's poisoned sword.

**King Claudius**  
Hamlet's uncle, now king of Denmark, who murdered his brother by pouring "juice of cursed hebenon" into his ear as he slept in the orchard, then married the widowed queen. A smooth hypocrite ("one may smile, and smile, and be a villain"), he privately admits "O, my offence is rank."

**Queen Gertrude**  
Hamlet's mother, "the queen, your husband's brother's wife," who married Claudius "within a month" of her husband's death ("O, most wicked speed"), which sickens her son ("Frailty, thy name is woman!"). She dies drinking the poisoned cup meant for Hamlet: "The drink, the drink! I am poison'd."

**Ophelia**  
Polonius's daughter, whom Hamlet loved ("forty thousand brothers Could not, with all their quantity of love, Make up my sum") but then cruelly rejects ("I loved you not... Get thee to a nunnery"). After her father's death she goes mad, singing ("He is dead and gone, lady") and handing out flowers ("There's rosemary, that's for remembrance"), and drowns when a willow branch breaks under her.

**Polonius**  
The long-winded, meddling old counsellor of the King (the edition's cast list calls him "lord chamberlain"), father of Laertes and Ophelia, who declares "brevity is the soul of wit" in a speech so roundabout that the Queen snaps "More matter, with less art." He gives Laertes advice such as "Neither a borrower nor a lender be" and "to thine ownself be true." Hamlet mutters "These tedious old fools!" behind his back. Spying "behind the arras," he is stabbed by Hamlet, who calls him "a foolish prating knave."

**Laertes**  
Polonius's hot-tempered son, who has been living in France. He storms the palace at the head of a rioting crowd to avenge his father ("O thou vile king, Give me my father!"). With Claudius he plots a fencing match using an unblunted sword that he coats in poison ("I'll anoint my sword"). Dying, he confesses: "I am justly kill'd with mine own treachery."

**Horatio**  
Hamlet's calm, loyal friend and fellow scholar ("Thou art a scholar; speak to it, Horatio"), the friend Hamlet means when he says "Give me that man That is not passion's slave." He wants to die with Hamlet ("I am more an antique Roman than a Dane"), but lives on to tell the story, bidding him "Good night, sweet prince."

**The Ghost** · *the ghost of Hamlet's father, the late King Hamlet*  
The dead king, who walks at night "Armed at point exactly, cap-a-pe" (in full armour), with a face "more in sorrow than in anger" and a beard "sable silver'd." He tells Hamlet he was poisoned by Claudius and demands: "Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder... remember me."

**Yorick** · *the skull*  
The late king's jester, dead for twenty-three years, whose skull the gravedigger digs up. Hamlet remembers him fondly: "Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio: a fellow of infinite jest."

**Rosencrantz and Guildenstern**  
Two courtiers who grew up with Hamlet and are summoned by the King to spy on him. Hamlet rewrites the King's letter so that "the bearers" are put "to sudden death" in England, hence the report: "Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are dead."

### 3. Don Quixote (Miguel de Cervantes)

**Don Quixote of La Mancha** · *Alonso Quixano (surname also given as Quixada or Quesada); "the Knight of the Rueful Countenance"*  
A country gentleman of La Mancha of small means (his plain meals eat up "three-quarters of his income"), "bordering on fifty," "spare, gaunt-featured," who reads so many chivalry romances that "his brains got so dry that he lost his wits." He dubs himself a knight-errant and charges at "thirty or forty windmills" he takes for "monstrous giants." Sancho nicknames him "the Knight of the Rueful Countenance." On his deathbed he regains his reason: "I am no longer Don Quixote of La Mancha, but Alonso Quixano."

**Sancho Panza**  
Don Quixote's squire, a neighbouring farm labourer, "an honest man ... but with very little wit in his pate," lured by the promise of an island to govern. In a picture described in the book he has "a big belly, a short body, and long shanks." He rides his donkey Dapple, strings proverbs together endlessly, and is made "governor of the island of Barataria" as a joke played on him by a duke and duchess.

**Dulcinea del Toboso** · *real name Aldonza Lorenzo*  
The imaginary "Lady of his Thoughts": in reality Aldonza Lorenzo, "a very good-looking farm-girl" from El Toboso whom Don Quixote once loved from afar ("she never knew it"). Sancho, who knows her, says she "can fling a crowbar as well as the lustiest lad in all the town."

**Rocinante** · *Don Quixote's horse*  
Don Quixote's bony old nag ("tantum pellis et ossa fuit," all skin and bone), which in his eyes surpasses Alexander's Bucephalus. He spends four days choosing the name, which reflects that he was "a hack before."

**Samson Carrasco** · *the Bachelor; "Knight of the Mirrors"; "Knight of the White Moon"*  
Bartholomew Carrasco's son, from Don Quixote's own village, a sharp-witted young bachelor just home from studying at Salamanca, "somewhere about four-and-twenty," with "a round face, a flat nose, and a large mouth, all indications of a mischievous disposition." He disguises himself as a knight to defeat Don Quixote and send him home: he loses as the "Knight of the Mirrors" but wins as the "Knight of the White Moon."

### 4. Pride and Prejudice (Jane Austen)

**Elizabeth Bennet** · *Lizzy, Eliza*  
The heroine and second of the five Bennet daughters, not yet twenty-one. Quick-witted and playful, with a "lively, playful disposition, which delighted in anything ridiculous"; her father's favourite for her quickness. Darcy is won over by "the beautiful expression of her dark eyes." Flattered by Wickham and offended by Darcy, she misjudges them both, until she admits: "Till this moment, I never knew myself."

**Mr. Fitzwilliam Darcy** · *Mr. Darcy*  
A very rich gentleman ("ten thousand a year") with a large estate, Pemberley, in Derbyshire; tall and handsome ("fine, tall person, handsome features, noble mien"). His cold manners make people think him "proud," and he snubs Elizabeth as merely "tolerable." Later he admits he has been "a selfish being all my life" and tells Elizabeth, "By you, I was properly humbled."

**Jane Bennet** · *Miss Bennet*  
The eldest Bennet sister, "almost three and twenty," and the beauty of the family: Bingley calls her "the most beautiful creature I ever beheld." Gentle and trusting to a fault; Elizabeth tells her, "You never see a fault in anybody." She falls quietly in love with Bingley.

**Mr. Charles Bingley**  
A wealthy, good-natured young man from the north of England ("four or five thousand a year") who rents Netherfield Park. He is "good looking and gentleman-like," with "easy, unaffected manners"; Darcy's close friend and Jane's admirer.

**Mr. Bennet**  
Elizabeth's dry, ironic father: "so odd a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice" that his wife has not understood him in twenty-three years of marriage. He mocks his wife's "nerves." His estate is entailed on a male heir, so after his death his cousin Mr. Collins "may turn you all out of this house."

**Mrs. Bennet**  
The girls' silly, excitable mother: "a woman of mean understanding, little information, and uncertain temper" who complains of her "poor nerves." "The business of her life was to get her daughters married."

**Lydia Bennet**  
The youngest sister, "a stout, well-grown girl of fifteen, with a fine complexion and good-humoured countenance," her mother's favourite, with "high animal spirits." She and her sister Catherine "could talk of nothing but officers"; she runs off with Wickham.

**Mr. George Wickham**  
A handsome, smooth-talking militia officer with "all the best part of beauty, a fine countenance, a good figure, and very pleasing address." He is the son of the Pemberley estate manager. Darcy reveals that Wickham once got Darcy's fifteen-year-old sister, Georgiana, "to consent to an elopement" in pursuit of her "thirty thousand pounds"; later he runs off with Lydia.

**Mr. William Collins**  
A pompous clergyman cousin who will inherit the Bennet estate: "a tall, heavy looking young man of five and twenty," "grave and stately" and "very formal." He is "a mixture of pride and obsequiousness, self-importance and humility" and fawns over his patroness Lady Catherine. Elizabeth turns down his proposal.

**Lady Catherine de Bourgh**  
Darcy's rich, overbearing aunt and Mr. Collins's patroness at Rosings Park: "a tall, large woman, with strongly-marked features, which might once have been handsome," whose manner reminds visitors of "their inferior rank." She storms at Elizabeth to stop her marrying Darcy ("Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?").

### 5. Frankenstein (Mary Shelley)

**Victor Frankenstein**  
A young Genevese scientist from one of the republic's leading families who, as a student at the University of Ingolstadt, discovers how to "infuse a spark of being into the lifeless thing" and builds a living man. He is a student, never called "Doctor." Horrified by what he has made, he runs from it ("I rushed out of the room"), and the Creature later kills his little brother, his best friend and his bride. When the explorer Walton finds him adrift "on a large fragment of ice," his eyes have "an expression of wildness, and even madness."

**The Creature** · *never named; called "daemon," "fiend," "wretch," "monster"*  
Victor's creation, built "about eight feet in height" with features chosen "as beautiful," but hideous when alive: "yellow skin" barely covering "muscles and arteries," flowing "lustrous black" hair, teeth "of a pearly whiteness," "watery eyes," a "shrivelled complexion and straight black lips." He has no name. By secretly listening to a cottager family he teaches himself to speak and read (Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, Werther); shut out by everyone ("Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded"), he turns to revenge: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend." He kills Victor's little brother William, then Clerval and Elizabeth. The book has no neck bolts, green skin or Igor.

**Elizabeth Lavenza**  
Victor's adopted "more than sister" and later his bride. In this 1831 text she is a foundling Victor's parents find in a poor cottage on "the shores of the Lake of Como": "thin, and very fair," with hair of "the brightest living gold" and "blue eyes cloudless." The Creature murders her on the wedding night, as he had threatened ("I shall be with you on your wedding night").

**Henry Clerval**  
Victor's boyhood best friend, "the son of a merchant of Geneva" and "a boy of singular talent and fancy" who loved "books of chivalry and romance." He nurses Victor through months of fever and is later murdered by the Creature.

**Robert Walton**  
An ambitious, lonely explorer ("I have no friend, Margaret") on "a voyage of discovery towards the northern pole," whose letters to his sister Margaret Saville in England frame the story. He takes Victor aboard from the ice, hears his tale, and finally agrees to turn back when his crew demands it.

### 6. A Christmas Carol (Charles Dickens)

**Ebenezer Scrooge**  
A miserly old London businessman: "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner," "solitary as an oyster," whom the story finds on Christmas Eve "busy in his counting house." The cold inside him shows in his "pointed nose," red eyes, "thin lips blue" and "grating voice"; "A frosty rime was on his head, and on his eyebrows." He answers Christmas greetings with "Bah!" and "Humbug!" and says that if the poor would rather die, "they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population." After the ghosts' visits he becomes "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man" as the city knew.

**Jacob Marley's Ghost** · *Marley*  
Scrooge's business partner, dead "seven years ago, this very night" ("Marley was dead, to begin with"). His ghost drags a long chain "of cash boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers, deeds, and heavy purses wrought in steel" ("I wear the chain I forged in life"), and warns Scrooge that three spirits will come.

**Bob Cratchit**  
Scrooge's poorly paid, good-hearted clerk, paid "fifteen shillings a week," who shivers by a fire so small "it looked like one coal" and wears a long comforter and "threadbare clothes." He still toasts "Mr. Scrooge, the Founder of the Feast!" at his family's Christmas dinner.

**Tiny Tim** · *Tim Cratchit*  
Bob's small, disabled son, who "bore a little crutch, and had his limbs supported by an iron frame" and says "God bless us every one!" The Ghost of Christmas Present warns that "the child will die" unless things change; in the end he "did not die," and Scrooge becomes "a second father" to him.

**The Ghost of Christmas Past** · *the first spirit*  
A strange figure "like a child; yet not so like a child as like an old man," with long white hair and an unwrinkled face. A "bright clear jet of light" springs from its head, and it carries "a great extinguisher for a cap." It shows Scrooge his youth, including his kind old employer Fezziwig and his fiancée (Belle), who releases him because "Another idol has displaced me... A golden one."

**The Ghost of Christmas Present** · *the second spirit*  
Seated on a throne of food, "a jolly giant, glorious to see," in "one simple deep green robe, or mantle, bordered with white fur," with a holly wreath and long "dark-brown curls," carrying a torch shaped like Plenty's horn. From the folds of his robe he brings out two "wretched, abject, frightful, hideous, miserable" children: "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want."

**The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come** · *the Phantom*  
A silent Phantom "shrouded in a deep black garment" that hides everything "save one outstretched hand." It never speaks and only points, showing Scrooge a death nobody mourns ("I don't know of anybody to go to it") and then his own grave.

**Fred** · *Scrooge's nephew*  
Scrooge's cheerful nephew, whose face is "ruddy and handsome" and whose "eyes sparkled." He invites his uncle to Christmas dinner despite his "Humbug!" and means "to give him the same chance every year, whether he likes it or not."

**Mr. Fezziwig** · *old Fezziwig*  
Scrooge's jolly former master, "an old gentleman in a Welsh wig," who treated his apprentices to a joyful Christmas Eve dance: the example of a generous employer.

### 7. Moby-Dick (Herman Melville)

**Ishmael** · *the narrator*  
The narrator ("Call me Ishmael."), a former country schoolmaster who goes to sea when he has "little or no money" and a "damp, drizzly November in my soul." He is the only survivor of the Pequod, kept afloat on a coffin until the ship Rachel picks him up.

**Captain Ahab**  
The Pequod's grim old captain, obsessed with killing the white whale that "reaped away" his leg. He stands on an ivory leg "fashioned from the polished bone of the sperm whale's jaw," his body looks "made of solid bronze," and a livid white scar runs from his grey hair down his "tawny scorched face and neck." Defiant to the point of blasphemy ("I'd strike the sun if it insulted me"), he is dragged to his death by his own harpoon line.

**Moby Dick** · *the White Whale*  
A sperm whale of "uncommon bulk," recognizable by "a peculiar snow-white wrinkled forehead, and a high, pyramidical white hump"; Ahab describes him as "a white-headed whale with a wrinkled brow and a crooked jaw" with three holes in his right tail-fluke. In the final chase he rams and sinks Ahab's ship.

**Queequeg**  
A tattooed harpooneer from Rokovoko (spelled Kokovoko in some other editions), an island "far away to the West and South" that "is not down in any map"; a "cannibal" who smokes a tomahawk-pipe, and Ishmael's closest friend. His face is "of a dark, purplish, yellow colour, here and there stuck over with large blackish looking squares," yet Ishmael sees in him "a simple honest heart." His coffin, turned into a life-buoy, saves Ishmael.

**Starbuck** · *chief mate*  
The chief mate, a Quaker from Nantucket: "a long, earnest man" whose flesh is "hard as twice-baked biscuit." Brave but careful ("I will have no man in my boat," he says, "who is not afraid of a whale"), he openly objects to Ahab's quest, calling revenge on "a dumb brute" madness.

**Stubb** · *second mate*  
The second mate, from Cape Cod: "A happy-go-lucky; neither craven nor valiant." He is calm and joking even in the thick of a whale hunt, and never without his "short, black little pipe."

**Flask** · *third mate*  
The third mate, from Tisbury on Martha's Vineyard: "A short, stout, ruddy young fellow, very pugnacious concerning whales," who seems to think they "had personally and hereditarily affronted him."

### 8. Les Misérables (Victor Hugo)

**Jean Valjean** · *prisoner number 24,601; "Father Madeleine"; M. Fauchelevent*  
A poor pruner who steals "a loaf of bread" for his widowed sister's seven children, is "condemned to five years in the galleys," and, after failed escapes, spends nineteen years there as "number 24,601." He is "thickset and robust," enormously strong ("worth four men"; he lifts a cart off a trapped man). The Bishop's mercy ("you no longer belong to evil, but to good") changes him. As "Father Madeleine" he builds up the town's black-glass trinket industry and becomes mayor, fetches Fantine's daughter Cosette from the Thénardiers and raises her, carries the wounded Marius through the sewers, and dies saying "I die happy."

**Inspector Javert**  
The relentless police inspector, "born in prison, of a fortune-teller, whose husband was in the galleys." His face is "a flat nose, with two deep nostrils, towards which enormous whiskers ascended on his cheeks." He lives by two ideas, "respect for authority, hatred of rebellion," and hunts Valjean for years. When Valjean spares his life, his certainties collapse and he throws himself into the Seine.

**Fantine**  
Cosette's mother, abandoned by her lover: "a lovely blonde, with fine teeth" ("her gold was on her head, and her pearls were in her mouth"). To pay the Thénardiers for her child's keep she sells her "admirable golden hair" for ten francs and her two front teeth, falls into prostitution, and dies in the infirmary.

**Cosette** · *"the Lark"*  
Fantine's daughter, left with the Thénardiers, who use her as their drudge: "nearly eight years old, but she seemed to be hardly six," "thin and pale," called "the Lark" because she was up "sweeping the street before daylight." "If she had been happy, she might have been pretty." Valjean takes her away from them and raises her; she grows into "a tall and beautiful creature" and marries Marius.

**Marius Pontmercy**  
A poor student ("Marius was poor, and his chamber was poverty-stricken"), "a handsome young man, of medium stature, with thick and intensely black hair, a lofty and intelligent brow." Taken from his father and raised by his grandfather as a "fanatical and severe" Royalist, he goes to law school, learns his father was a colonel made a baron by the Emperor on the battlefield, falls in love with Cosette in the Luxembourg gardens, and is badly wounded at the barricade.

**Bishop Myriel** · *Monseigneur Bienvenu*  
The saintly Bishop of Digne, "an old man of about seventy-five years of age," who swaps his palace for the cramped hospital next door ("you have my house, and I have yours") and keeps only "one thousand livres" of his "salary of fifteen thousand francs" for himself. He lives with his sister, Mademoiselle Baptistine. When the ex-convict Valjean steals his silver, he gives him his "two large candlesticks of massive silver" as well and tells him he has bought his soul for God.

**The Thénardiers** · *later alias "Jondrette"*  
Greedy, cruel innkeepers who keep "a sort of cook-shop" at Montfermeil and later live in Paris under false names. Thénardier is "a small, thin, pale, angular, bony, feeble man, who had a sickly air and who was wonderfully healthy," with "the glance of a polecat." His wife is "tall, blond, red, fat, angular, square, enormous," with a big red-blotched face and "a beard," who boasts she can "crack a nut with one blow of her fist." They are the parents of Éponine and Gavroche.

**Éponine**  
The Thénardiers' eldest daughter, secretly in love with Marius. Dressed "in men's clothes" at the barricade, she puts her hand over the muzzle of a gun aimed at him and dies saying "I believe that I was a little bit in love with you."

**Gavroche**  
A cheeky Paris street boy, "eleven or twelve years of age," the neglected son of the Thénardiers ("his mother did not love him"). He fights at the barricade and is shot while collecting cartridges under fire, singing "C'est la faute à Voltaire."

**Enjolras**  
The rich, idealistic young leader ("the priest of the ideal") of the Friends of the A.B.C., a group made up mostly of students, who commands the barricade: "a charming young man, who was capable of being terrible. He was angelically handsome."

### 9. Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (Lewis Carroll)

**Alice**  
A curious, well-mannered little girl who, "burning with curiosity," follows a rabbit underground and keeps growing and shrinking after drinking and eating things labelled "Drink Me" and "Eat Me." She tries to curtsey even while falling down the rabbit-hole, talks to herself constantly (she "generally gave herself very good advice" but "very seldom followed it"), misses her cat Dinah, and finally wakes up from "such a curious dream." The book never gives her age or hair colour. All it says is that her hair "doesn't go in ringlets at all"; the blonde hair and blue dress come from illustrations and films.

**The White Rabbit** · *W. Rabbit*  
A nervous, fussy "White Rabbit with pink eyes" who wears a waistcoat, checks his pocket watch and cries "Oh dear! Oh dear! I shall be late!" He carries white kid gloves and a fan, has a housemaid called Mary Ann (he mistakes Alice for her), and acts as the herald at the trial.

**The Cheshire Cat**  
The Duchess's cat, a large cat "grinning from ear to ear" that can appear and vanish at will, once fading away until only its grin is left ("a grin without a cat!"). It tells Alice, "we're all mad here. I'm mad. You're mad."

**The Hatter** · *never called "the Mad Hatter" in the book*  
A rude, riddling hat-seller ("I keep them to sell ... I'm a hatter") who hosts the "Mad Tea-Party" with the March Hare and the Dormouse and greets Alice with "Your hair wants cutting." He asks the unanswered riddle "Why is a raven like a writing-desk?" He has quarrelled with Time, so tea-time never ends: "It's always six o'clock now." The book never calls him "the Mad Hatter," and the "10/6" price tag is only in the pictures.

**The March Hare**  
The Hatter's tea-party partner. Alice hopes that "as this is May it won't be raving mad," but it offers her wine when there is only tea ("Have some wine").

**The Dormouse**  
A sleepy guest at the tea party, "fast asleep" between the Hatter and the March Hare, who use it as a cushion. It wakes to tell a story about sisters who live in a "treacle-well," and when Alice leaves they are "trying to put the Dormouse into the teapot."

**The Queen of Hearts**  
The furious playing-card queen who "had only one way of settling all difficulties, great or small": "Off with his head!" She plays croquet with live flamingoes as mallets and hedgehogs as balls. Her death sentences are never carried out: the Gryphon says "they never executes nobody," and the King quietly pardons everyone "in a low voice."

**The Caterpillar**  
The book introduces him as "a large blue caterpillar," sitting on a mushroom "with its arms folded, quietly smoking a long hookah," who asks Alice in a "languid, sleepy voice": "Who are you?" It tells her that one side of the mushroom makes you taller and the other shorter.

**The Duchess**  
A "very ugly" noblewoman with a pepper-filled kitchen and a howling baby that turns into a pig in Alice's arms. Later she turns oddly affectionate ("you dear old thing!") and insists "Everything's got a moral, if only you can find it."

**The Mock Turtle**  
A tearful creature, "the thing Mock Turtle Soup is made from," whom Alice finds "sighing as if his heart would break"; with the Gryphon he tells of his school in the sea and performs the Lobster Quadrille.

### 10. Crime and Punishment (Fyodor Dostoevsky)

**Rodion Romanovitch Raskolnikov** · *Rodya*  
An ex-student in St. Petersburg, "crushed by poverty," "exceptionally handsome, above the average in height, slim, well-built, with beautiful dark eyes and dark brown hair," but dressed in rags. His article divides people into "ordinary" and "extraordinary" men who may overstep the law. He kills an old pawnbroker and her sister Lizaveta with an axe, falls into fever and "delirium," finally confesses, and is sentenced to eight years of penal servitude in Siberia.

**Sonia Marmeladov** · *Sofya Semyonovna*  
Marmeladov's timid, devout daughter ("What should I be without God?"), who has taken "a yellow ticket" (become a registered prostitute) to feed her family: "a small thin girl of eighteen with fair hair, rather pretty, with wonderful blue eyes." Raskolnikov has her read him the raising of Lazarus from her New Testament; she urges him to confess ("say to all men aloud, 'I am a murderer!'") and follows "the party of convicts" to Siberia.

**Porfiry Petrovitch**  
The shrewd investigating lawyer, "about five and thirty, short, stout even to corpulence, and clean shaven," with "a large round head" and a "soft, round, rather snub-nosed face." He plays cat-and-mouse with Raskolnikov ("Have you seen a butterfly round a candle?"), is sure of his guilt without proof, and openly advises him to "surrender and confess."

**Dounia Raskolnikov** · *Avdotya Romanovna*  
Raskolnikov's proud, devoted sister: "remarkably good-looking; she was tall, strikingly well-proportioned, strong and self-reliant," with dark brown hair and "almost black eyes." She nearly marries the official Luzhin for her brother's sake ("I won't have your sacrifice, Dounia"), holds off Svidrigailov with a revolver, and marries Razumihin.

**Razumihin** · *Dmitri Prokofitch*  
Raskolnikov's loyal university friend, "good-natured to the point of simplicity," "extremely intelligent, though he was certainly rather a simpleton at times," "tall, thin, black-haired and always badly shaved," and reputed to be "of great physical strength." He looks after Raskolnikov's family and marries Dounia.

**Arkady Svidrigailov**  
The landowner in whose house Dounia was a governess and was "insulted"; he comes to Petersburg after her ("with designs on my sister," as Raskolnikov puts it). His face is "like a mask; white and red, with bright red lips, with a flaxen beard," and his eyes are "somehow too blue." He leaves money to Sonia, and after Dounia rejects him he shoots himself.

**Semyon Zaharovitch Marmeladov**  
Sonia's father, a drunken retired government clerk, "a man over fifty, bald and grizzled," his face "bloated from continual drinking" and of "a yellow, even greenish, tinge." He has sold even his wife's stockings "for drink" and drinks the money his daughter earns; he is run over by a carriage.

**Katerina Ivanovna** · *Marmeladov's wife*  
Marmeladov's consumptive wife and Sonia's stepmother: "a rather tall, slim and graceful woman, terribly emaciated, with magnificent dark brown hair and with a hectic flush in her cheeks."

**Alyona Ivanovna** · *the old pawnbroker*  
The moneylender Raskolnikov murders: "a diminutive, withered up old woman of sixty, with sharp malignant eyes and a sharp little nose," her grizzled hair "thickly smeared with oil."

### 11. War and Peace (Leo Tolstoy)

**Pierre Bezukhov** · *Count Pyotr Kirillovich Bezukhov*  
The awkward, kind-hearted hero: "a stout, heavily built young man with close-cropped hair, spectacles," whose smile gives him "a childlike, kindly, even rather silly look." The "illegitimate son of Count Bezúkhov," he unexpectedly inherits the title and a huge fortune, is married off to the beautiful Elen, shoots Dolokhov in a duel over her, joins the Freemasons, and is taken prisoner by the French in 1812. There the peasant Platon Karataev, "kindly, and round," helps him rebuild his faith in life "on new and unshakable foundations," and he finally marries Natasha.

**Natasha Rostova** · *Natalya Ilyinichna Rostova*  
The lively heroine, first seen at thirteen as a "black-eyed, wide-mouthed girl, not pretty but full of life" with black curls. She loves to sing and dance (she joins in a Russian folk dance at "Uncle's"). Engaged to Prince Andrey, she is seduced by Anatole Kuragin and nearly runs off with him. She nurses the dying Andrey, and in the Epilogue she is Pierre's wife, "a strong, handsome, and fertile woman."

**Prince Andrey Bolkonski** · *Prince Andrew, Andrei*  
Pierre's disillusioned friend: "a very handsome young man, of medium height, with firm, clearcut features" and a "weary, bored expression." Wounded at Austerlitz, he stares up at "the lofty sky" and decides "All is vanity, all falsehood, except that infinite sky." After his wife dies he becomes engaged to Natasha, is fatally wounded in 1812, and dies with Natasha beside him.

**Nikolay Rostov** · *Nicholas Rostov*  
Natasha's hot-headed brother, a hussar officer: "short with curly hair and an open expression," whose face "expressed impetuosity and enthusiasm." He loses forty-three thousand rubles to Dolokhov at cards, and in 1813 marries Princess Marya, takes up farming, and pays off all his debts within four years.

**Princess Marya Bolkonskaya** · *Princess Mary, Maria*  
Andrey's devout, gentle sister, plain-looking but with "large, deep and luminous" eyes so beautiful "that very often in spite of the plainness of her face" they made her more attractive than beauty. She lives under her strict old father at Bald Hills and later marries Nikolay Rostov.

**Elen Kuragina** · *Hélène; later Countess Elena Vasilievna Bezukhova*  
Prince Vasili's dazzling daughter, "the beautiful Elèn," with white shoulders and "the smile of a perfectly beautiful woman." She marries the newly rich Pierre and is rumoured to be Dolokhov's lover. Pierre "knew she was very stupid," yet society thinks her "a lovely and clever woman."

**Sonya** · *the Rostovs' orphan niece*  
Nikolay's orphaned cousin, raised by the Rostovs, who loves him and whom he promises to marry: "a slender little brunette" with long lashes and thick black braids "coiling twice round her head." When his family needs him to marry an heiress, she writes to "release him from his promise and set him completely free," and stays on as a "sterile flower."

**Fedor Dolokhov**  
A reckless officer, "a notorious gambler and duelist" of medium height "with curly hair and light-blue eyes." He wins a bet by drinking a bottle of rum sitting on a third-floor window ledge, is rumoured to be Elen's lover, and is wounded by Pierre in a duel.

**General Kutuzov** · *Mikhail Kutuzov, the Russian commander-in-chief (historical)*  
The old Russian commander-in-chief, blind in one eye ("the bleached eyeball, the scar") and ever more "corpulent, flaccid, and fat." Tolstoy shows his wisdom as patience rather than brilliance: "not storming and attacking but patience and time are wanted."

**Napoleon** · *Napoleon Bonaparte (historical)*  
The French emperor, shown without glamour: a short man with a "rotund stomach," "the fat thighs of his short legs," a "plump white neck" and "plump little hand," who smells of "Eau de cologne."

### 12. Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (Mark Twain)

**Huckleberry "Huck" Finn** · *the narrator*  
The barely schooled but resourceful runaway son of a violent drunk (he can "spell and read and write just a little"), who tells the story in his own dialect. He hates being "sivilized" by the Widow Douglas, fakes his own murder to escape his father, and rafts down the Mississippi with Jim, even dressing up as a girl to gather news. His conscience tells him to turn Jim in, but he decides to help him anyway: "All right, then, I'll go to hell." At the end he plans to "light out for the Territory."

**Jim**  
Miss Watson's enslaved man, who runs away when he overhears she means to "sell me down to Orleans" for "eight hund'd dollars." On the raft he becomes Huck's warm, protective friend ("you's de bes' fren' Jim's ever had"), full of folk signs and superstitions; he keeps Huck from seeing the face of a dead man who turns out to be Huck's father. He dreams of buying his wife and children out of slavery and grieves over once striking his little daughter, not knowing she was deaf. It turns out that Miss Watson "set him free in her will."

**Tom Sawyer**  
Huck's show-off friend, whose head is full of adventure books (he blames "enchantment" and cites Don Quixote). He starts "Tom Sawyer's Gang" of make-believe robbers and turns freeing Jim into a needlessly complicated game, although he knows Jim is already free. He ends up with "a bullet in the calf of his leg" and wears it on a watch-guard.

**Pap Finn** · *Huck's father*  
Huck's violent, drunken father, who "used to lay drunk with the hogs in the tanyard" and "used to always whale me when he was sober"; "most fifty," with long, tangled, greasy hair and a sickly face, "a tree-toad white, a fish-belly white," dressed in rags. He is later found dead in a house floating down the river.

**The King and the Duke** · *two con men*  
Two swindlers who take over the raft. The "King" is "about seventy or upwards" with "a bald head and very gray whiskers" and claims to be "the late Dauphin" of France; the Duke is "about thirty" and claims descent from the "Duke of Bridgewater." They stage fake shows like "The Royal Nonesuch," cheat a grieving family, and sell Jim "for forty dollars," until a town tars and feathers them.

**Widow Douglas and Miss Watson**  
The two sisters who take Huck in. The kind widow wants to "sivilize" him; her sister, Miss Watson, "a tolerable slim old maid, with goggles on," drills him with a spelling-book and is Jim's owner.

### 13. The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes (Arthur Conan Doyle)

**Sherlock Holmes**  
The detective of 221B Baker Street, "the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen," with a mind that finds emotions "abhorrent." Tall and gaunt, with a "hawk-like nose" and "long, thin fingers"; he smokes a black clay pipe ("quite a three pipe problem"), is "an enthusiastic musician," swings between "cocaine and ambition," and is a master of disguise (a drunken groom, a clergyman). He wears a "close-fitting cloth cap," but the word "deerstalker" never appears, and he never says "Elementary, my dear Watson."

**Dr. Watson** · *the narrator*  
Holmes's "intimate friend and associate" and the narrator of the stories: a doctor who has "returned to civil practice" and married, so he now sees less of Holmes. He still carries "the Jezail bullet" from his "Afghan campaign." Holmes relies on him ("someone with me on whom I can thoroughly rely"). His first name is not given in this collection, and in "The Man with the Twisted Lip" his wife calls him "James."

**Irene Adler** · *"the woman"*  
An opera singer ("Born in New Jersey in the year 1858. Contralto ... Prima donna Imperial Opera of Warsaw") and the woman who outwits Holmes, so that to him "she is always the woman." The King of Bohemia says she has "the face of the most beautiful of women, and the mind of the most resolute of men." A trained actress, she sees through Holmes's disguise, disguises herself in men's clothes, marries the lawyer Godfrey Norton and slips away.

**Inspector Lestrade** · *Lestrade, of Scotland Yard*  
The Scotland Yard detective, whom Watson describes as "A lean, ferret-like man, furtive and sly-looking." Self-satisfied about his own theories, he scoffs at Holmes's methods, yet refers puzzling cases to him. In "The Boscombe Valley Mystery" he insists the son is the killer, and the real murderer later confesses to Holmes.

**Mrs. Hudson** · *the landlady*  
Holmes's landlady, who gets woken early for clients and lights the fire for them. In "A Scandal in Bohemia" the landlady is called "Mrs. Turner" instead.

### 14. Dracula (Bram Stoker)

**Count Dracula** · *the Count*  
A centuries-old nobleman ("I am boyar") and vampire whose castle lies where "Transylvania, Moldavia and Bukovina" meet. Van Helsing believes he was once "that Voivode Dracula who won his name against the Turk." Harker first sees "a tall old man, clean shaven save for a long white moustache," dressed all in black, with an arched nose, bushy eyebrows that almost meet, "peculiarly sharp white teeth" over very red lips, ears "extremely pointed," hair on his palms, long sharp nails and bad breath. Drinking blood makes him look younger; in London he looks like "a tall, thin man, with a beaky nose and black moustache and pointed beard." He casts no reflection, crawls face-down down his castle wall, commands rats, bats and wolves, and can come as mist. Daylight weakens him but does not kill him. He dies from Jonathan's knife across the throat and Quincey's bowie knife in the heart, not from a stake.

**Jonathan Harker**  
A young Englishman, newly qualified as a solicitor ("my examination was successful; and I am now a full-blown solicitor!") sent to Transylvania to handle the Count's purchase of a London house, Carfax. Held prisoner in the castle, he escapes, marries Mina, and joins the hunt. The strain turns his "dark brown hair" white overnight. He cuts the Count's throat with his Kukri knife.

**Mina Harker** · *Wilhelmina Murray, Jonathan's fiancée then wife*  
An assistant schoolmistress who learns shorthand and typing to help Jonathan, and who pulls the group's diaries together. Van Helsing says she has "man's brain" and "a woman's heart." Dracula makes her drink his blood, and the holy Wafer Van Helsing lays on her forehead burns a scar into it ("Unclean, unclean!").

**Lucy Westenra** · *the "bloofer lady"*  
Mina's lovely, lively friend, a sleepwalker since childhood, who gets three marriage proposals in one day ("Why can't they let a girl marry three men...?") and accepts Arthur Holmwood. Drained by Dracula, she becomes a vampire, the "bloofer lady" who lures children, until Arthur drives a stake through her heart.

**Professor Abraham Van Helsing**  
A doctor from Amsterdam ("the Dutchman," as Quincey calls him), Seward's old teacher: "a philosopher and a metaphysician, and one of the most advanced scientists of his day" with "an absolutely open mind." Mina describes him as strongly built, clean-shaven, with reddish hair and "big, bushy brows"; "Big, dark blue eyes are set widely apart." He is the one who tells the others what vampires are and how to fight them.

**R. M. Renfield**  
A patient in Dr. Seward's asylum, aged 59, of "great physical strength" and "morbidly excitable," whom Seward calls a "zoöphagous (life-eating) maniac": he feeds flies to spiders and spiders to birds and eats them to absorb their lives. He worships Dracula as "the Master," and is killed with a broken back after turning against him.

**Dr. John Seward**  
A clever young doctor, "only nine-and-twenty," who runs "an immense lunatic asylum" next door to Carfax and records his diary on a phonograph. One of Lucy's suitors, and Van Helsing's former pupil.

**Arthur Holmwood** · *later Lord Godalming*  
Lucy's fiancé, "the Hon. Arthur Holmwood, only son of Lord Godalming," whose title he inherits. He has to drive the stake into the vampire Lucy himself.

**Quincey P. Morris**  
Lucy's suitor "number Two" on the day she gets three proposals, a young, fresh-faced adventurer, "an American from Texas." He plunges his bowie knife into Dracula's heart but is fatally wounded and dies "a gallant gentleman." Mina and Jonathan Harker's son, born on the day he died, is named after him: "we call him Quincey."

### 15. The Great Gatsby (F. Scott Fitzgerald)

**Jay Gatsby** · *born James Gatz*  
A mysterious, very rich young man, "a year or two over thirty," who throws huge parties at his Long Island mansion, calls everyone "old sport," and has a smile "with a quality of eternal reassurance in it." He was born James Gatz of North Dakota, a poor farmer's son, and invented "Jay Gatsby" from "his Platonic conception of himself." His money comes from bootlegging (selling "grain alcohol over the counter"). He wears a pink suit, claims to be "an Oxford man," and stares at "the green light at the end of Daisy's dock," believing you can repeat the past ("Why of course you can!"). George Wilson shoots him in his pool.

**Nick Carraway** · *the narrator*  
The narrator, Gatsby's neighbour: a Yale graduate ("New Haven in 1915") from a well-off Middle Western family who comes East to "learn the bond business." He is "inclined to reserve all judgements" and calls himself "one of the few honest people that I have ever known." He is Daisy's second cousin once removed, and turns thirty during the story.

**Daisy Buchanan** · *born Daisy Fay*  
Gatsby's lost love, once "the most popular of all the young girls in Louisville," now Tom's wife. Her face is "sad and lovely with bright things in it," and Gatsby says "Her voice is full of money." She hopes her daughter will be "a beautiful little fool." Driving Gatsby's car, she runs down Myrtle, then lets Gatsby take the blame. Nick concludes that she and Tom "were careless people."

**Tom Buchanan**  
Daisy's rich, brutal husband, a former Yale football star: "a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner" and "a cruel body." He spouts racist theories ("The Rise of the Coloured Empires"), keeps a mistress, Myrtle, breaks her nose with his open hand, and points the grieving Wilson toward Gatsby.

**Jordan Baker**  
Daisy's friend and a famous golf champion, "a slender, small-breasted girl, with an erect carriage." Nick briefly dates her but finds her "incurably dishonest": rumour says she once moved her golf ball "from a bad lie" in a tournament.

**Myrtle Wilson**  
Tom's mistress, wife of the garage-owner George Wilson: "in the middle thirties, and faintly stout, but she carried her flesh sensuously." She runs into the road and is killed by Gatsby's car.

**George Wilson**  
Myrtle's husband, who runs a run-down garage ("unprosperous and bare") in the "valley of ashes": "a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome." Half-mad with grief, he shoots Gatsby and then himself.

**Meyer Wolfshiem** · *spelled Wolfsheim in later editions*  
Gatsby's shady business partner, "a gambler," who with Gatsby bought up "side-street drugstores" to sell "grain alcohol over the counter," and who is said to be "the man who fixed the World's Series back in 1919" and wears cuff buttons made of human teeth ("Finest specimens of human molars").

## Easy reads for English learners (up to B1, easiest first)

### E1. The Tale of Peter Rabbit and other tales (Beatrix Potter)

**Peter Rabbit**  
The naughty one of four little rabbits, "Flopsy, Mopsy, Cottontail, and Peter," who live with their mother under "a very big fir-tree." She tells them not to go into Mr. McGregor's garden: "Your Father had an accident there; he was put in a pie by Mrs. McGregor." Peter, "who was very naughty," runs straight there and squeezes under the gate. Mr. McGregor chases him; Peter loses both shoes and leaves behind his "blue jacket with brass buttons, quite new," hides in a can full of water, sneezes ("Kertyschoo!"), and at last slips out under the gate. At home he is ill and gets camomile tea, while Flopsy, Mopsy, and Cottontail have "bread and milk and blackberries for supper." In a later tale he goes back with his cousin Benjamin Bunny to get his clothes, and when he grows up he keeps "a nursery garden."

**Mr. McGregor**  
The gardener whose vegetable garden the rabbits keep raiding; his wife once put Peter's father "in a pie." When he finds Peter among his vegetables, he runs after him "waving a rake" and shouting "Stop thief!", tries to catch him under a sieve, and afterwards hangs Peter's jacket and shoes on a scarecrow "to frighten the blackbirds." In *The Tale of the Flopsy Bunnies* he finds Benjamin's children asleep on his rubbish heap and drops them into a sack ("six leetle rabbits!"), meaning to "sell them and buy myself baccy." But a mouse nibbles a hole in the sack, their parents fill it with rotten vegetable marrows, and Mrs. McGregor becomes "very very angry."

**Benjamin Bunny**  
Peter's cousin, "little Benjamin Bunny," who is "perfectly at home" in Mr. McGregor's garden, because he goes there with his father "to get lettuces for their Sunday dinner." He takes Peter back to get his clothes from the scarecrow ("the proper way to get in is to climb down a pear-tree") and fills Peter's handkerchief with onions as a present for his aunt. When they meet a cat, he hides them both under a basket, and the cat sits on it "for five hours," until his father, old Mr. Benjamin Bunny, jumps on the cat, locks it in the greenhouse, and whips his son "with the little switch." When he grows up he marries "his Cousin Flopsy," and their children are the Flopsy Bunnies.

**Squirrel Nutkin**  
A little red squirrel whose story is "a Tale about a tail." He and his brother Twinkleberry and their cousins sail to Owl Island on rafts, using their tails as sails, to gather nuts, and each day the others bring a present for the owl, Old Brown. But "Nutkin was excessively impertinent in his manners": he brings nothing, dances about, and sings rude riddles at the owl. On the sixth day he jumps "right onto the head of Old Brown!" The owl catches him and is about to skin him, but Nutkin pulls so hard that "his tail broke in two," and he escapes. Ever since, if you ask him a riddle, "he will throw sticks at you."

**Mrs. Tiggy-Winkle**  
A washerwoman who lives in a tiny kitchen inside the hill above Little-town. The little girl Lucie, looking for her lost "pocket-handkins," finds her ironing: "a very stout short person" in a print gown and apron, whose "little black nose went sniffle, sniffle, snuffle," with prickles under her cap. "I'm an excellent clear-starcher!" she says. She washes for all the animals: Cock Robin's "little scarlet waistcoat," Squirrel Nutkin's "red tailcoat with no tail," Peter Rabbit's "very much shrunk blue jacket." After tea she carries the clean clothes down the hill, then runs off without waiting for thanks, and Lucie sees that "Mrs. Tiggy-winkle was nothing but a Hedgehog."

**Mr. Jeremy Fisher**  
A frog who lives "in a little damp house amongst the buttercups at the edge of a pond" and likes getting his feet wet. On a rainy day he puts on "a macintosh, and a pair of shiny goloshes" and goes fishing from a boat made of a lily-leaf, hoping to catch minnows for dinner with his friends "Mr. Alderman Ptolemy Tortoise and Sir Isaac Newton." Instead he catches "little Jack Sharp the stickleback, covered with spines," and then a huge trout grabs him and dives, but spits him out because it does not like the taste of his macintosh; it swallows only his goloshes. "What a mercy that was not a pike!" His friends come to dinner anyway and eat "a roasted grasshopper with ladybird sauce."

**Jemima Puddle-Duck**  
A farm duck who "was annoyed because the farmer's wife would not let her hatch her own eggs." Wearing "a shawl and a poke bonnet," she flies to a wood to make a secret nest and meets "an elegantly dressed gentleman reading a newspaper," with "black prick ears and sandy coloured whiskers" and "a long bushy tail." He lends her a shed full of feathers, where she lays nine eggs. When he asks her to bring sage, thyme, mint, parsley and onions for "a savoury omelette," she still does not suspect him, although these are the herbs used "for stuffing roast duck": "Jemima Puddle-duck was a simpleton." The farm collie, Kep, saves her with two foxhound puppies, but the puppies eat her eggs. Later she is allowed to keep some eggs, "but only four of them hatched."

### E2. Aesop's Fables (Aesop)

**The Hare and the Tortoise**  
A Hare mocks a Tortoise "for being so slow upon his feet," so the Tortoise challenges him to a race. The Hare runs so far ahead that he lies down "and fell fast asleep," while "the Tortoise kept plodding on" and wins. The moral: "Slow and steady wins the race."

**The Fox** · *in "The Fox and the Grapes" and "The Fox and the Crow"*  
Aesop's clever fox. When he cannot reach some grapes, he walks off "with an air of dignity and unconcern" and says "they are quite sour," which is where the saying about sour grapes comes from. When he sees a Crow with a piece of cheese, he "set his wits to work": he praises her beauty and wonders whether her voice is as sweet, so she caws and drops the cheese. Then he tells her, "You have a voice, madam, I see: what you want is wits."

**The Shepherd's Boy** · *the boy who cried wolf*  
A Shepherd's Boy who thinks it "great fun to hoax the villagers" by shouting "Wolf! wolf!" when there is none. When a Wolf really comes, nobody believes him, and it kills "sheep after sheep." The moral: "You cannot believe a liar even when he tells the truth."

**The Grasshopper and the Ants**  
In winter a starving Grasshopper begs some busy Ants for food. She has saved nothing, because "I was so busy singing that I hadn't the time." The Ants answer: "If you spent the summer singing... you can't do better than spend the winter dancing."

**The Lion and the Mouse**  
A Lion catches a Mouse that ran over his face but lets it go, laughing at its promise to repay him. Later the Lion is caught "in a net which had been spread for game by some hunters," and the Mouse gnaws through the ropes: "even a Mouse can help a Lion."

**The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse**  
The Town Mouse sneers at his friend's simple "dinner of barleycorns and roots" and takes him to town, to a larder full of "flour and oatmeal and figs and honey and dates." But someone comes into the larder, and they have to hide in "a narrow and exceedingly uncomfortable hole," so the Country Mouse goes home: "You live in the lap of luxury, I can see, but you are surrounded by dangers."

**The Goose That Laid the Golden Eggs**  
A Man and his Wife own "a Goose which laid a Golden Egg every day," but, thinking they are "not getting rich fast enough," they kill it to take the gold inside. It turns out to be "just like any other goose." The moral: "Much wants more and loses all."

### E3. The Happy Prince and Other Tales (Oscar Wilde)

**The Happy Prince**  
A statue on "a tall column" high above the city, "gilded all over with thin leaves of fine gold," with two sapphires for eyes and a ruby on his sword-hilt. As a living prince he lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci, "where sorrow is not allowed to enter." Now he can see "all the ugliness and all the misery of my city," and he weeps. He sends the Swallow to give away his ruby (to a poor seamstress with a sick little boy), then his two sapphire eyes (to a young writer too cold to finish his play, and to a little match-girl), then his gold "leaf by leaf," until he is blind and "quite dull and grey." When the Swallow dies, the Prince's lead heart snaps in two. The town melts the statue down, but the broken heart will not melt, and God's Angel chooses it as one of "the two most precious things in the city."

**The Swallow**  
A little bird who stays behind when his friends fly to Egypt, "for he was in love with the most beautiful Reed" (he soon gets tired of her: "She has no conversation"). Stopping for the night between the statue's feet, he is hit by the Prince's tears. Again and again the Prince asks, "Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow," and he stays "one night longer" to carry the ruby, the sapphires and the gold to the poor. When the Prince is blind, he promises, "I will stay with you always." He grows "colder and colder" but will not leave, and at last he kisses the Prince on the lips and falls "dead at his feet." At the end God says that "in my garden of Paradise this little bird shall sing forevermore."

**The Selfish Giant**  
A giant who comes home after seven years with "his friend the Cornish ogre" and finds children playing in his "large lovely garden." He builds a high wall with a noticeboard, "Trespassers Will Be Prosecuted." "He was a very selfish Giant." After that, spring never comes to his garden: "So it was always Winter there." When the children creep back in through a hole in the wall, spring comes back with them, except in one corner, where a little boy is too small to climb a tree. "How selfish I have been!" says the Giant. He lifts the boy into the tree, the boy kisses him, and he knocks down the wall. Years later, old and weak, he finds the same boy under a tree "quite covered with lovely white blossoms," with the prints of two nails on his hands and feet: "these are the wounds of Love." That afternoon the children find the Giant "lying dead under the tree, all covered with white blossoms."

**The Nightingale**  
A nightingale who hears a young Student say that a girl will dance with him "if I brought her red roses," but there is no red rose in his garden. "Here at last is a true lover," she decides. The rose-tree under the Student's window tells her the only way to make one: "You must sing to me with your breast against a thorn," all night, until the thorn pierces her heart. "Death is a great price to pay for a red rose," she says, but "Love is better than Life." She sings until the rose is finished and is found "lying dead in the long grass, with the thorn in her heart." The Professor's daughter refuses the rose because "the Chamberlain's nephew has sent me some real jewels," and the Student throws it into the gutter, where "a cartwheel went over it."

### E4. The Adventures of Pinocchio (Carlo Collodi)

**Pinocchio**  
A wooden puppet that Geppetto carves from a piece of wood that can already cry and laugh. His nose grows while it is still being carved ("no sooner had he made it than it began to grow"), and later it grows whenever he lies to the Fairy: "his nose, which was already long, grew at once two inches longer." Lazy and easily tricked, he is robbed by the Fox and the Cat, runs off to the "Land of Boobies" with Candlewick, and wakes up with "a magnificent pair of donkey's ears." He finds Geppetto inside the Dogfish ("Oh, my dear papa! I have found you at last!") and at the end becomes "a well-behaved little boy."

**Geppetto** · *nicknamed "Pudding"*  
"A lively little old man" whom the neighbourhood boys tease as "Pudding," because "his yellow wig greatly resembled a pudding made of Indian corn"; he is "very fiery" and flies into a rage at the name. He is so poor that the fire in his room is only painted on the wall. He carves Pinocchio and sells his old coat to buy him a spelling-book. At sea he is "swallowed by the terrible Dogfish," where Pinocchio finds him.

**The Talking-Cricket**  
An old cricket who has "lived in this room a hundred years or more" and warns Pinocchio about disobedient boys. Pinocchio, angry at being called a puppet with "a wooden head," throws a hammer at him, and the Cricket is left "dried up and flattened against the wall." He comes back as "the ghost of the Talking-Cricket" to warn Pinocchio again. The name "Jiminy" comes from the Disney film.

**The Fairy with Blue Hair** · *first seen as "a beautiful Child"*  
When Pinocchio runs from the assassins, "a beautiful Child" appears at a window: "She had blue hair and a face as white as a waxen image." As "the little Fairy with blue hair" she cares for him "with all the patience of a good mamma," tricks him into taking his medicine with sugar, and shows him that lies are easy to spot, because some lies "have long noses."

**The Fox and the Cat**  
Two swindlers Pinocchio meets on the road: "a Fox lame of one foot, and a Cat blind of both eyes." Both are faking, because at the sound of his gold the Fox "stretched out the paw that seemed crippled, and the Cat opened wide two eyes." They tell him to bury his coins in "the Field of Miracles" so they will grow. Disguised as assassins in charcoal sacks, they hang him from the Big Oak. At the end they are begging, and the Cat "had so long feigned blindness that she had become blind in reality."

**Fire-Eater** · *the showman*  
The owner of the puppet theatre, "very big, and so ugly that the sight of him was enough to frighten anyone," with a beard "as black as ink" so long that "he trod upon it when he walked." He looks wicked but is soft-hearted: when he feels pity he sneezes ("The showman has sneezed and that is a sign that he pities you"). He spares Pinocchio and gives him "five gold pieces" for Geppetto.

**Candlewick** · *real name Romeo*  
Pinocchio's favourite schoolfellow, called Candlewick "because he was so thin, straight and bright, like the new wick of a little nightlight," and "the laziest and the naughtiest boy in the school." He persuades Pinocchio to come to the "Land of Boobies," where both boys turn into donkeys; Candlewick is sold to a peasant.

**The Dogfish**  
A huge sea monster that "has been spreading devastation and ruin" and swallows Geppetto and later Pinocchio. Inside it, Pinocchio finds his father, and they escape while "the Dogfish is sleeping like a dormouse." In the book it is a dogfish, not a whale.

### E5. Winnie-the-Pooh (A. A. Milne)

**Winnie-the-Pooh** · *Pooh; "Edward Bear"*  
Christopher Robin's bear: "Edward Bear, known to his friends as Winnie-the-Pooh, or Pooh for short." He loves honey ("the only reason for making honey is so as I can eat it"), makes up little hums, and calls himself "a Bear of Very Little Brain, and long words Bother me." He floats up to a bees' nest on a balloon, trying "to look like a small black cloud," and eats so much at Rabbit's that he gets stuck in the front door, "a Wedged Bear in Great Tightness." To Christopher Robin he is "the Best Bear in All the World." Tigger is not in this book; he first appears in the sequel.

**Piglet**  
Pooh's small, timid friend, who "lived in a very grand house in the middle of a beech-tree" beside a broken sign reading "Trespassers W," which he says was his grandfather's name. He admits "It is hard to be brave... when you're only a Very Small Animal." When a flood leaves him "Entirely Surrounded by Water," he sends a message in a bottle, and Christopher Robin and Pooh sail to him in "The Brain of Pooh."

**Eeyore**  
"The Old Grey Donkey," who "stood by himself in a thistly corner of the forest" and is always gloomy ("I don't seem to have felt at all how for a long time"). He loses his tail, which Pooh finds hanging on Owl's door as a "bell-rope"; Christopher Robin nails it back on. For his birthday he gets "a Useful Pot to Keep Things In" and a balloon that Piglet has burst on the way.

**Owl**  
The wise-seeming bird who lives at "The Chestnuts, an old-world residence of great charm." He can "read and write and spell his own name Wol," but "went all to pieces over delicate words like measles and buttered toast," so Christopher Robin writes his door notices. He likes long words, such as "the customary procedure," and has been using Eeyore's lost tail as his "Handsome bell-rope."

**Rabbit**  
The forest's organiser, with a whole crowd of "friends-and-relations." Pooh gets stuck in his front door. When Kanga arrives he writes a "Plan to Capture Baby Roo," which starts from the facts that "Kanga runs faster than any of Us, even Me."

**Kanga and Baby Roo**  
A mother kangaroo and her baby who suddenly appear in the forest. To Rabbit she is "a Strange Animal": "An animal who carries her family about with her in her pocket!" Rabbit's plan swaps Piglet for Roo in Kanga's pocket, but Kanga pretends not to notice and gives Piglet a bath instead.

**Christopher Robin**  
The boy who owns Pooh and to whom the stories are told (the narrator calls him "you"). He is "the only one in the forest who could spell," calls Pooh "silly old Bear" "in such a loving voice," leads an "Expotition to the North Pole," and rescues Piglet from the flood.

### E6. Grimms' Fairy Tales (Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm)

**Hansel and Grethel** · *Hänsel and Gretel*  
The children of "a poor woodcutter." When "great scarcity fell on the land," his wife persuades him to leave them in the forest. Hansel first marks the way home with "white pebbles" that shine "like real silver pennies," but on the next trip birds eat his trail of bread crumbs. Lost, they find a house "built of bread and covered with cakes" whose windows "were of clear sugar." The witch who lives there fattens Hansel to eat him, but he holds out "a little bone" instead of his finger, and Grethel pushes her into the oven: "Grethel gave her a push that drove her far into it."

**Little Redcap** · *Little Red Riding Hood*  
A little girl whose grandmother gives her "a little cap of red velvet, which suited her so well that she would never wear anything else," and so she is called Little Redcap. Sent with "a piece of cake and a bottle of wine" to her sick grandmother, she meets the wolf, who reaches the house first and swallows both of them after the famous questions ("what big ears you have!"... "The better to eat you with!"). A passing huntsman cuts the wolf open with "a pair of scissors" and both come out alive; they fill the wolf with "great stones," and he falls dead.

**Snow-White** · *Snow White*  
A princess "as white as snow, and as red as blood," with hair "as black as ebony." Her proud stepmother asks her mirror, "Looking-glass, Looking-glass, on the wall, Who in this land is the fairest of all?" and tries to kill her. Snow-White hides in the dwarfs' cottage with its "seven little beds." She does not wake with a kiss: the men carrying her coffin stumble, and "the poisonous piece of apple" comes out of her throat. At the wedding the stepmother must "put on the red-hot shoes, and dance until she dropped down dead."

**Cinderella**  
A girl whose stepsisters make her work in the kitchen "from morning till night" and sleep "by the fireside in the ashes." There is no fairy godmother and no glass slipper. She plants a hazel twig on her mother's grave, and a bird in the tree throws down her ball dresses when she calls, "Shiver and quiver, little tree, Silver and gold throw down over me." The prince has the stairs smeared with pitch, catches her "left slipper," which is "all golden," and one stepsister even cuts off her toe to fit it. At the wedding, pigeons peck out the stepsisters' eyes.

**Rapunzel**  
A girl locked by an enchantress in a tower that "had neither stairs nor door." Her "magnificent long hair, fine as spun gold" is the only way up: "Rapunzel, Rapunzel, Let down thy hair to me." When the enchantress finds out about the king's son, he leaps from the tower and "the thorns into which he fell, pierced his eyes." Years later Rapunzel finds him, and "Two of her tears wetted his eyes and they grew clear again."

**Rumpelstiltskin**  
A little man who appears when a boastful miller claims his daughter "can spin straw into gold" and the King shuts her in with the straw. He spins it for her, the third time in return for a promise of "your first child." When she, now Queen, finally guesses "Perhaps your name is Rumpelstiltskin?", he drives his foot deep into the earth in his rage and pulls at his leg so hard that "he tore himself in two."

**The Frog-King** · *the frog prince*  
A frog who brings back a princess's "golden ball" from a well after she promises to be his companion. When he creeps up to her in bed and asks to be lifted up, she does not kiss him: "she... threw him with all her might against the wall," and he turns into "a King's son with beautiful kind eyes." His servant, Faithful Henry, had been so unhappy that he had "three iron bands" put round his heart.

### E7. Heidi (Johanna Spyri)

**Heidi** · *named after her mother, Adelheid*  
An orphan girl, "scarcely five years old" when her aunt Deta takes her up the mountain and leaves her with her grandfather: her father Tobias was killed by a falling beam, and her mother Adelheid died soon after. On the Alm she sleeps in "a hayloft, which was filled with fresh and fragrant hay," and goes up to the pasture with Peter and the goats. Taken to Frankfurt as a companion for the sick girl Clara, she "can't read" at first, and grows "so homesick that her little body has wasted away" that she walks in her sleep, until a doctor sends her home, where she is "so happy to be home again." Back on the Alm she reads to Peter's blind grandmother and helps Clara learn to walk.

**The Alm-Uncle** · *Heidi's grandfather*  
Heidi's grandfather, a gruff old man living alone up on the Alm. He was once "heir to a large farm in Domleschg" but "lost everything with drink and play." The villagers are afraid of him, "with those thick grey eyebrows and that huge uncanny beard," but he takes Heidi in and later carries Clara up the mountain.

**Peter the goatherd**  
The village boy who takes the goats up to the pasture each day. He would rather cut hazel-rods than "learn to read" and lives with his mother and "an old blind grandmother." Jealous when Clara takes up all of Heidi's time, he pushes Clara's empty rolling-chair down the slope: "He had destroyed the chair to get rid of the stranger."

**Clara Sesemann**  
A girl in Frankfurt, "a sick child" who spends her days "in a comfortable rolling-chair," with "a pale, thin face with soft blue eyes." Heidi is brought to keep her company. Later Clara visits the Alm, and there she takes her first steps: "I can take steps, one after another."

**Miss Rottenmeier**  
The strict housekeeper of the Sesemann house in Frankfurt, who "wore a peculiar uniform with a long cape, and a high cap on her head" and runs the whole household. She is shocked that Heidi has studied no books at all.

**Peter's grandmother**  
Peter's old, blind grandmother, whom Heidi visits in the village. Heidi saves "soft white rolls" for her because "the black bread is too hard," and once Heidi can read, she reads her songs from her hymnbook.

### E8. The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (L. Frank Baum)

**Dorothy**  
A little girl who lives on the gray Kansas prairie with Uncle Henry, "who was a farmer," and Aunt Em. A cyclone carries her house to the Land of Oz, where it falls on the Wicked Witch of the East, and Dorothy is given the Witch's "silver shoes with pointed toes." In her gingham dress "with checks of white and blue" and a pink sunbonnet, she follows the road "paved with yellow brick" to the Emerald City to ask the Wizard to send her home. She melts the Wicked Witch of the West with "the bucket of water," and finally clicks her heels together three times: "Take me home to Aunt Em!" The book never gives her a surname, and her shoes are silver; the ruby slippers come from the film.

**Toto** · *Dorothy's dog*  
Dorothy's dog, "a little black dog, with long silky hair and small black eyes that twinkled merrily." In gray Kansas, "It was Toto that made Dorothy laugh." In the Wizard's throne room he jumps away from the Lion's roar and tips over a screen, uncovering the real Wizard.

**The Scarecrow**  
A scarecrow whose head is "a small sack stuffed with straw, with eyes, nose, and mouth painted on it." Dorothy lifts him off his pole in a cornfield. "I am stuffed, so I have no brains at all," he says, so he goes to ask Oz for some. The Wizard fills his head with bran mixed with pins and needles ("bran-new brains"), and he ends up as "the ruler of the Emerald City."

**The Tin Woodman**  
Found in the forest with his axe raised, "a man made entirely of tin" who cannot move: "They are rusted so badly that I cannot move them at all," so he begs for an oilcan. He was once a woodchopper; after the Wicked Witch of the East enchanted his axe, it cut off his legs and arms one after another, and a tinsmith replaced each with tin. He wants a heart, and the Wizard gives him one "made entirely of silk and stuffed with sawdust." Glinda sends him to "rule the Winkies."

**The Cowardly Lion**  
A lion everyone expects to be brave, since "the Lion is everywhere thought to be the King of Beasts," but who covers his fear by roaring: "I'm such a coward." He joins Dorothy because "my life is simply unbearable without a bit of courage." The Wizard gives him a green-gold dish to "Drink," explaining that "courage is always inside one." Later the forest animals accept him as "King of the Forest."

**The Wizard of Oz** · *"Oz, the Great and Terrible"*  
The ruler of the Emerald City, who announces himself as "Oz, the Great and Terrible" and receives each visitor in a different shape: "an enormous Head, without a body," "a most lovely Lady," a Beast, a Ball of Fire. Behind the screen he is "a little old man, with a bald head and a wrinkled face," born in Omaha, who was a ventriloquist and then a balloonist. He admits "I am a humbug" and "a very good man, but I'm a very bad Wizard." He made the city look green by making everyone wear green spectacles, and he leaves in his balloon.

**The Wicked Witch of the West**  
The witch who rules the West, "where the Winkies live," and "would make you her slave if you passed her way." She "had but one eye, yet that was as powerful as a telescope," commands the Winged Monkeys through her Golden Cap, and is "too much afraid of the dark" to steal Dorothy's shoes at night. When Dorothy throws water over her she melts "like brown sugar": "Didn't you know water would be the end of me?"

**Glinda** · *the Good Witch of the South*  
"The Witch of the South," "the most powerful of all the Witches," who "rules over the Quadlings." She is "both beautiful and young," with hair "a rich red in color" and blue eyes, and she tells Dorothy that the silver shoes can carry her anywhere: "All you have to do is to knock the heels together three times." In the book she is the Witch of the South; the kindly Witch who meets Dorothy first is the Witch of the North, who kisses her forehead.
