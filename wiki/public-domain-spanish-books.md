# Public Domain Spanish Books — Easy Vocabulary, Witty Language

A curated reading list of **public-domain full novels in Spanish** chosen for **A2-level vocabulary
with genuinely witty prose**, weighted toward **Peninsular** Spain, with translations into Spanish
labelled separately. No fables, no fairytales. The central finding: the books that actually work at
A2 are the **annotated student editions** — real novels published for American Spanish classes with
an introduction, footnotes and a full Spanish-English glossary bound in — and Project Gutenberg has
kept several of them.

## The honest problem with this brief

A2 + full novel + public domain is a tight intersection, and it is worth saying why before the list.
Public domain in practice means published before roughly 1930. Spanish prose from that era carries
grammar that no longer appears in speech: the `-ra`/`-se` imperfect subjunctive everywhere, the
pretérito anterior, enclitic pronouns stuck onto conjugated verbs (`díjole`, `fuése`), and the
`vosotros` forms. None of that is hard *conceptually* at A2 — it is just unfamiliar, and it arrives
in the first paragraph.

So a book here is A2 in **vocabulary and sentence length**, which is what was asked for. It is not
A2 in **register**. The annotated editions in Tier 1 are the fix: they gloss exactly the words a
first-year student stops on.

The other honest note: "easy" and "witty" pull against each other. Wit in Spanish is usually carried
by an ironic narrator, and ironic narrators use subordinate clauses. Where a book is easy but flat,
or witty but hard, this list says so rather than splitting the difference.

**Difficulty** below is A2 → B2, judged on vocabulary load and sentence length.
**Wit** is rated ●○○ to ●●●.
**Length**: Very short (<100pp) · Short (100–200) · Medium (200–350) · Long (350+).

## Tier 1 — Start here: annotated student editions

These are the reason this list is possible. Each is the real, unabridged Spanish text with an English
introduction, explanatory notes and a Spanish-English vocabulary at the back — published by Ginn,
D. C. Heath and similar houses for first- and second-year US college classes, and now free.

### 1. El Capitán Veneno — Pedro Antonio de Alarcón (1881)
**A2 · Wit ●●● · Very short · [PG 29731](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29731)**

The best first book on this list. A ferocious, woman-hating artillery captain is shot in a Madrid
street riot and carried into the house of a widow and her daughter, who proceed to nurse him and
dismantle him. It is essentially a two-hander: almost all dialogue, short sentences, comic timing
built on a man losing an argument for a hundred pages. The Gutenberg edition carries notes, a
vocabulary **and exercises** — it was built for exactly this use.

*Why it works at A2:* dialogue-driven prose has the shortest sentences and the most present tense in
19th-century Spanish. You are reading people talking, not a narrator describing.

### 2. El sombrero de tres picos — Pedro Antonio de Alarcón (1874)
**A2+ · Wit ●●● · Short · [PG 29506](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29506)**

A farce, and the funniest thing here. A miller, his very beautiful wife, a corrupt magistrate in the
three-cornered hat of office, a night of mistaken identity, wet clothes and swapped beds. Alarcón
narrates it like a man telling an anecdote in a bar — brisk chapters, a wink to the reader, no
sentimentality. Later became Falla's ballet. The Bourland student edition supplies introduction,
notes and vocabulary.

*Caveat:* slightly more narration than *El Capitán Veneno*, hence A2+. Chapters are very short, which
makes it forgiving.

### 3. José — Armando Palacio Valdés (1885)
**A2+ · Wit ●●○ · Short · [PG 27738](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/27738)**

A young fisherman in an Asturian village, his boat, his mother-in-law-to-be, and the sea. Palacio
Valdés wrote the cleanest, least ornamented prose of any Spanish novelist of his generation — this is
the plainest Spanish in Tier 1. The humour is affectionate and observational, aimed at village
characters, rather than the sustained comic machinery of the Alarcóns. Edited student edition.

### 4. Doña Perfecta — Benito Pérez Galdós (1876)
**B1 · Wit ●●○ (dark) · Medium · [PG 15725](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15725)**

The edition is the point: Athenaeum Press / Ginn, introduction and notes by A. R. Marsh, vocabulary by
Steven T. Byington, and it states outright that it was prepared for students *in the second half of
their first year*. That is a publisher's promise about difficulty, made in 1897 and still good.

The novel: a liberal engineer from Madrid arrives in a provincial town to marry his cousin, and the
town — personified by his aunt, Doña "Perfecta" — destroys him. The wit is entirely in the title and
the narrator's straight face; Galdós never once says the pious are monstrous, he just reports them.
It is the most *ironic* book in Tier 1 and the least *funny*. It ends badly.

> ⚠️ **Don't grab PG 2462** — that's the English translation. You want **15725**.

## Tier 2 — Plain Spanish texts, no glossary, still readable

No editorial apparatus. Keep a dictionary open.

### 5. La hermana San Sulpicio — Armando Palacio Valdés (1889)
**B1 · Wit ●●● · Medium · [PG 31013](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31013)**

The funniest novel in Tier 2. A solemn Galician doctor narrates his own pursuit of a Sevillian nun who
has left her order, and the joke is consistently on him — a northerner drowning in Andalusian
sunlight, wit and social choreography he cannot keep up with. Comic set-pieces, excellent dialogue.

*Caveat:* Palacio Valdés renders Andalusian pronunciation phonetically in dialogue (dropped `-s`,
`-d-` swallowed). Fun once you hear it, briefly bewildering on page one.

### 6. Juanita la Larga — Juan Valera (1895)
**B1 · Wit ●●● · Medium · [PG 16484](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16484)**

Andalusian village comedy: an illegitimate girl with more intelligence than anyone around her manages
a whole town's hypocrisy and marries exactly who she intends to. Valera is the most *elegant* wit in
Spanish of the period — dry, worldly, never raising his voice. Warmer and more comic than his
*Pepita Jiménez*, and a shade easier.

### 7. Misericordia — Benito Pérez Galdós (1897)
**B1+ · Wit ●●○ · Medium · [PG 21831](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/21831)**

Benina, a servant, secretly begs on Madrid's church steps to keep her ruined mistress in the illusion
of respectability. Galdós at his warmest, and full of superb low-life dialogue and Madrid street
speech. The humour is human rather than satirical.

*Caveat:* the beggars' slang and Almudena's broken Spanish are deliberately non-standard — atmospheric,
but not vocabulary you should be trying to learn.

### 8. Insolación — Emilia Pardo Bazán (1889)
**B1+ · Wit ●●● · Short · [PG 52597](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/52597)** *(bundled with Morriña)*

A respectable Madrid widow goes to the San Isidro fair, drinks too much manzanilla in the sun, and
spends the rest of the novel negotiating with her own conscience about what happened. Sharp, funny,
sexually frank for 1889, and short.

*Caveat:* Pardo Bazán has the richest vocabulary of anyone in Tier 2 — she is a naturalist and names
everything precisely. The wit is worth the dictionary work; the density is real.

### 9. El amigo Manso — Benito Pérez Galdós (1882)
**B2 · Wit ●●● · Medium · [PG 55563](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55563)**

Opens with the narrator announcing that he does not exist — he is a fiction, and he knows it. A
philosophy professor, rational and self-satisfied, watches everyone he tries to educate outmanoeuvre
him. The most *modern*-feeling book here and the most metafictional.

*Placed at B2 deliberately.* The wit is syntactic — it lives in how sentences are built — so it is the
one book here where simplifying the language would destroy the joke. Save it; don't start with it.

### 10. Pepita Jiménez — Juan Valera (1874)
**B2 · Wit ●●● · Short · [PG 17223](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17223)**

A seminarian writes home about his spiritual progress while falling completely in love with the young
widow his father intends to marry, and the comedy is in the gap between what he reports and what is
obviously happening. Epistolary, so the register is high and self-consciously literary — that is the
joke, and also why it is hard. One of the genuinely great witty novels in Spanish.

> ⚠️ **PG 30236 ("Pepita Ximenez") is the English translation.** You want **17223**.

## Tier 3 — Easy prose, but not witty

Listed because they are the most *readable* Spanish here, and you may want that on a bad week. None is
funny; do not go in expecting it.

- **La barraca** — Blasco Ibáñez, 1898 · **A2+/B1** · [PG 14944](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14944)
  Vigorous, concrete, short-sentenced prose — mechanically among the easiest adult Spanish written.
  A family tries to farm cursed land outside Valencia and the community destroys them. Grim throughout.
  Also: [Cañas y barro](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57781) (57781),
  [Arroz y tartana](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16413) (16413),
  [Flor de mayo](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/41746) (41746).
- **Marianela** — Galdós, 1878 · **B1** · [PG 17340](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17340)
  Short, emotionally direct, the standard "easy Galdós" set in schools. An ugly orphan girl guides a
  blind boy who has never seen her. It is tender and it is sad and there is no wit in it at all.

## Tier 4 — Worth knowing, with real caveats

- **Vida de Lazarillo de Tormes** — anonymous, 1554 · **Wit ●●● · Very short** · [PG 320](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/320)
  The origin of the picaresque and still genuinely funny — a starving boy serves a sequence of
  increasingly fraudulent masters and narrates it deadpan. Under 100 pages, first-person, concrete
  vocabulary, short sentences. **But it is 1554 Spanish**: archaic spelling and forms throughout. The
  Gutenberg text is unmodernised. Read it in a modernised print edition, or later.
- **Zalacaín el aventurero** — Pío Baroja, 1908 · **A2+/B1** · [PG 13264](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13264)
  Baroja wrote the shortest, plainest sentences of any major Spanish novelist — mechanically this is
  close to the easiest adult prose in the language, and the book is a brisk Basque adventure. Dry
  rather than witty. **Copyright caveat below.**
  Also: [El árbol de la ciencia](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60464) (60464, bleaker, more
  philosophical), [Mala hierba](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/43017) (43017).

## Translations into Spanish

Clearly labelled as requested. These are **not** Spanish literature — they are foreign works in
Spanish translation, and the prose you learn from is the translator's.

### Cándido, o El Optimismo — Voltaire, 1759
**B1 · Wit ●●● · Very short · [PG 7109](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7109)**

The strongest wit-per-page ratio on this entire list. Short declarative sentences, self-contained
episodes of three or four pages, relentless satire, and a plot that never slows down. The episodic
structure is ideal for a learner — finish a chapter, feel finished. If you want *funny* above all
else and can accept that it isn't Spanish, start here.

### La isla del tesoro — R. L. Stevenson, 1883, tr. Manuel Caballero
**B1 · Wit ●○○ · Medium · [PG 45438](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45438)**

Clear narrative Spanish, strong forward momentum, a story you may already know — which is the real
argument for it, since known plot carries you over unknown vocabulary. Adventure, not comedy.

**Not found, despite looking:** no Gutenberg Spanish edition could be confirmed for Verne's *La vuelta
al mundo en ochenta días*, Swift's *Los viajes de Gulliver*, Twain's *Tom Sawyer*, or Daudet's
*Tartarín de Tarascón* — the obvious witty candidates. Their Gutenberg entries are French, English or
Dutch. Spanish editions of these exist commercially but are modern, copyrighted translations.

## Copyright status — and one trap

Spain applies **life + 80 years** to authors who died **before 7 December 1987** (transitional
provisions preserving acquired rights under the 1879 law), and life + 70 otherwise. This is longer
than the EU norm and it catches one author on this list.

| Author | Died | Public domain in Spain from |
|---|---|---|
| Alarcón | 1891 | ~1972 |
| Valera | 1905 | ~1986 |
| Pérez Galdós | 1920 | ~2001 |
| Pardo Bazán | 1921 | ~2002 |
| Blasco Ibáñez | 1928 | ~2009 |
| Palacio Valdés | 1938 | ~2019 |
| **Pío Baroja** | 1956 | **~2037 — still under copyright in Spain** |

Everything in Tiers 1–3 is unambiguously public domain worldwide. **Baroja is the exception**:
Gutenberg is a US host clearing his pre-1930 books under US rules, but he remains in copyright in
Spain and across much of the EU. Downloading for personal reading is not the issue; redistributing is.

## Where to get the files

- **Project Gutenberg** — every link above. EPUB, Kindle and plain text per title. *Note: gutenberg.org
  was unreachable from the environment this research was done in, so links were verified through
  catalogue metadata rather than opened.*
- **LibriVox** — 600–700+ free public-domain Spanish audiobooks, volunteer-read, including Alarcón,
  Galdós and Pardo Bazán. Reading and listening to the same text is the single highest-leverage thing
  an A2 reader can do with 19th-century prose, because it resolves the unfamiliar verb forms into
  sound. Per-title availability was not individually confirmed.
- **Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes** (cervantesvirtual.com) — the Spanish-hosted alternative,
  strong on scholarly editions.

## Suggested order

1. **El Capitán Veneno** — glossed, very short, all dialogue, actually funny.
2. **El sombrero de tres picos** — glossed, farcical, short chapters.
3. **Cándido** in Spanish — if you want the wit dialled up and don't mind leaving Spain.
4. **La hermana San Sulpicio** or **Juanita la Larga** — first books without a glossary.
5. **Pepita Jiménez** / **El amigo Manso** — the payoff, once the register stops fighting you.

## What this list does not claim

Difficulty and wit ratings are editorial judgement from knowledge of these works plus verified edition
metadata — not measured readability scores. No passage was sampled, because the text repositories were
blocked from this environment. Ebook IDs, languages and the existence of notes/glossaries **were**
verified. See [[raw/2026-09-04_public-domain-spanish-books-research.md]] for the raw findings and the
method's limits.
